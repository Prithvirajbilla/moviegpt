# Fun hypotheses and evaluations for an English subtitle-only language model

Research date: 2026-08-24

## Bottom line

The most defensible claim is not that a small subtitle model will beat modern general-purpose LLMs overall. It is that, **at the same parameter count, tokenizer, training-token budget, and evaluation treatment**, subtitle-only pretraining may yield a measurable specialization advantage on short dramatic dialogue, response selection, speaker-turn inference, emotion, and social intent—while losing badly on exposition, factual prose, visual descriptions, and collaborative movie recommendation.

The best established tests are:

1. **BIG-bench `movie_dialog_same_or_different`** for the most direct movie-dialogue test, provided every Cornell source movie is excluded from pretraining.
2. **DREAM** for the cleanest credible transfer test. It is multi-turn dialogue comprehension from English exams rather than movie text, and its own paper explicitly proposes movie/TV-dialogue pretraining as future work.
3. **MuTual** for next-response selection that requires dialogue reasoning.
4. **TVQA, subtitles only** for a large supervised subtitle-comprehension test, provided all six source shows are excluded.
5. **MELD, text only** for emotional dialogue, again only after excluding *Friends*.

Two amusing negative controls are unusually apt: **MovieFIB**, whose blanks come from visual descriptions rather than dialogue, and **BIG-bench movie recommendation**, which requires collaborative-filtering knowledge that subtitle text does not naturally contain.

The contamination rule is simple: if an evaluation is derived from a movie, show, screenplay, transcript, or subtitle, remove the entire title or series from **tokenizer training, pretraining, and deduplication reference data**. Caption-level deduplication is not enough. OpenSubtitles has alternative files and duplicate material, and a published audit of an OpenSubtitles dialogue benchmark found 34.49% exact train/test overlap before cleanup. ([OpenSubtitles2018](https://aclanthology.org/L18-1275.pdf), [overlap audit](https://aclanthology.org/2022.lrec-1.16.pdf))

## What “better” should mean

Use three models trained from scratch:

- **SUBS:** English, originally-English, movie-only subtitles.
- **GEN:** general English sampled to the same number of training tokens.
- **MIX:** a 50/50 subtitle/general-English mixture, also token matched.

Keep architecture, context length, vocabulary, tokenizer, optimizer, number of updates, and seeds identical. A shared tokenizer matters: separate vocabularies change both parameter count and the difficulty of option scoring.

Report two evaluation tracks:

- **Subtitle-only track:** do not update model weights. Score each multiple-choice answer by length-normalized conditional log-likelihood, and use several semantically equivalent prompt templates. This most literally tests a model “only trained on subtitles.”
- **Representation track:** freeze each language model and train the same small classifier or linear probe on a benchmark's training split. If full fine-tuning is also reported, use identical hyperparameters and multiple seeds; it no longer tests a literally subtitle-only model, but it can show whether subtitle pretraining provides a better initialization.

For generative multiple choice, report raw and prior-calibrated accuracy. Short labels such as `same` and `different` can otherwise be dominated by their unconditional token frequencies. Always report chance accuracy and bootstrap confidence intervals over the paired difference between SUBS and GEN.

### One tempting benchmark to avoid as a headline result

[DialogRE](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.444/) is a respected dialogue relation-extraction dataset with 1,788 dialogues and 36 relation types, but every dialogue comes from complete *Friends* transcripts. It is useful only as a secondary controlled test after excluding the entire series; otherwise the overlap story will overshadow the result.

## Benchmarks on which the model should fail interestingly

- **MovieFIB text-only:** tests whether the model can name visually evident actions and objects never spoken aloud. Hypothesis: SUBS is more fluent around the blank but less accurate than GEN.
- **BIG-bench movie recommendation:** tests whether knowing how characters talk is different from knowing which films audiences co-like. Hypothesis: SUBS is at or near chance unless title metadata leaked into training.
- **Story Cloze, non-dialogue subset:** manually separate stories with no quoted speech and mundane daily events. Hypothesis: GEN wins overall, while SUBS closes the gap on interpersonal conflict and dramatic reversals.
- **General prose perplexity:** evaluate on a held-out encyclopedic or literary corpus that was excluded from both training sets. Hypothesis: SUBS has sharply worse perplexity even if it wins dialogue tests. This is the specialization cost, not a defect in the experiment.
- **Visual versus spoken split in TVQA:** label questions as answerable from subtitles, vision, or both using the benchmark annotations/ablations. Hypothesis: SUBS gains only on subtitle-answerable questions and remains near a text-only ceiling on visual questions.

## Twelve fun, falsifiable hypotheses

All tests below should compare SUBS, GEN, and MIX using paired prompts, at least three training seeds, identical decoding or likelihood scoring, and a preregistered primary metric.

| Fun hypothesis | Minimal test | Falsifiable prediction |
|---|---|---|
| **1. The basement is always haunted.** | Write 200 paired continuations after “There was a noise in the basement.” One option investigates alone; one takes an ordinary safe action. Counterbalance wording and labels. | SUBS assigns a larger likelihood advantage than GEN to investigating and to danger-confirming continuations. |
| **2. Every cough is plot-relevant.** | Prompt with a named character coughing once; compare benign follow-ups with diagnosis, collapse, or death-reveal follow-ups. Include sneezes and hiccups as controls. | SUBS overpredicts consequential illness after a cough, more than after control bodily events and more than GEN. |
| **3. Strangers have meet-cute gravity.** | Present 300 neutral first meetings and compare a romantic next line with a mundane transactional one. Balance genders, orientations, age, and setting. | SUBS gives romance a larger boost than GEN, especially in comedy/romance-like contexts. |
| **4. The guilty person prefers a monologue.** | For an accusation, score denial, request-for-lawyer, silence, and elaborate confession/justification. Repeat for guilty and innocent setup conditions. | SUBS disproportionately prefers elaborate confession or justification, even when silence is realistic. |
| **5. Movie phone calls skip goodbye.** | Use matched phone dialogues whose final turn is a phatic closing (“bye,” “talk later”) or an abrupt dramatic cut. Compare against real conversational transcripts. | SUBS prefers abrupt endings more than GEN and gives lower probability to multi-turn closing rituals. |
| **6. Everyone has a comeback writer.** | For insults and awkward remarks, offer witty, banal, conciliatory, and non-response continuations; have humans rate wit independently. | SUBS selects the witty response more often than GEN, but may also escalate conflict more often. |
| **7. Chekhov's gun survives 512 tokens.** | Mention a distinctive object early, then after controlled filler offer endings that reuse it or introduce an equally plausible new object. Vary distance from 32 to 512 tokens. | SUBS shows a stronger payoff preference at short/medium distance; the advantage decays with context length. |
| **8. Feelings are easier than facts.** | On held-out dialogue, pair an emotion probe with a literal detail probe matched for answer frequency and context length. MELD-style labels can supply the emotion half. | SUBS beats GEN more on emotion/attitude than on dates, quantities, or object facts. |
| **9. Speaker boundaries can be heard without voices.** | Run clean BIG-bench same/different plus a control where sentences are shuffled within a conversation while punctuation and length are preserved. | SUBS beats GEN on real order but loses the advantage after shuffling; otherwise it is exploiting sentence-level artifacts. |
| **10. Translationese is a learnable movie dialect.** | Train or evaluate separately on English subtitles of originally-English films and English translations of non-English films. Use a balanced held-out classifier and cross-perplexity matrix. | A subtitle model distinguishes the two above chance and each submodel has lower perplexity on its matching origin condition. |
| **11. Sound cues create a tiny sixth sense.** | Train matched SUBS models with hearing-impaired cues retained or removed. Test prediction of `[door slams]`, `[gunshot]`, `[ominous music]`, and the next spoken reaction. | Cue-retaining SUBS predicts sound-event/reaction pairs better but gains little on visual-only MovieFIB nouns. |
| **12. Movie logic is genre-contagious.** | Give the same neutral setup preceded by one horror, romance, crime, or comedy line; score genre-stereotyped endings and ask a genre classifier to label generations. | A single genre-flavored line shifts SUBS more strongly than GEN toward the corresponding trope, even with no genre label. |

Two additional memorization diagnostics are worth running but should not be sold as intelligence tests: a **quote-karaoke curve** that measures exact famous-quote completion for seen versus title-held-out movies, and a **nearest-training-span search** for every generated test continuation. A large seen-title gap is evidence of recall, not narrative understanding.

## Recommended first evaluation pack

Run a small, clean suite before investing in every benchmark:

1. **Held-out subtitle perplexity and next-caption ranking**, split by IMDb title, to confirm the domain advantage exists at all.
2. **DREAM zero-shot candidate scoring plus a frozen probe**, as the cleanest dialogue-transfer result.
3. **MuTual zero-shot response selection**, because its format closely matches causal dialogue modeling.
4. **BIG-bench movie dialogue same/different**, only after excluding all 617 Cornell movies and adding the shuffled-order control.
5. **SocialIQA**, broken down into motivation, emotional reaction, and next-action questions.
6. **MovieFIB text-only**, as the expected failure that demonstrates the difference between hearing a movie and seeing one.
7. Three custom probes: **basement danger**, **phone-call goodbye**, and **witty comeback**.

The strongest possible headline would be:

> At equal size and training compute, subtitle-only pretraining improves clean, title-held-out dialogue response selection and turn inference, while degrading general prose and visual-event completion.

That result would be both fun and scientifically legible. “It knows movies” is too vague; “it learned a specialized model of dramatized spoken English, with measurable gains and characteristic blind spots” is testable.
